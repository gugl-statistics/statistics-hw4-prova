1Ex. 1 - Welford's recurrence formulas

Updating the mean, the sum of squares and the variance one observation at a time.

We have a stream of observations x1, x2, … and after the first k of them we want

Mk = (1/k) Σi=1..k xi,    Sk = Σi=1..k (xi − Mk)²,    s²k = Sk / (k − 1)

without storing the old observations and without going through them again. Sk is the sum of squared deviations (SS) and s²k is the sample variance (with the k − 1 denominator).

Step 0: Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Since k·Mk = (k − 1)·Mk−1 + xk, dividing by k:

Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1) / k

The idea is to never compute the big sum Σxi, but only a correction to the previous mean: the new point "pulls" the mean towards itself by 1/k of the distance. For the rest I call this distance

δk = xk − Mk−1,   so   Mk = Mk−1 + δk/k

Two consequences that I use below:

  1. xk − Mk = δk − δk/k = δk(k − 1)/k
  2. Σi=1..k−1 (xi − Mk−1) = 0 (deviations from the mean always sum to zero)

Step 1: recurrence for the sum of squares Sk

Same approach: write the new quantity in terms of the old one plus a correction. Split Sk into the first k − 1 terms and the last one, and in the first k − 1 terms replace Mk = Mk−1 + δk/k:

Sk = Σi=1..k−1 (xi − Mk−1 − δk/k)² + (xk − Mk)²

    = Σi=1..k−1 (xi − Mk−1)² − (2δk/k)·Σi=1..k−1 (xi − Mk−1) + (k − 1)·δk²/k² + δk²(k − 1)²/k²

    = Sk−1 − 0 + δk²·(k − 1)/k² · (1 + (k − 1))

    = Sk−1 + δk²·(k − 1)/k

The middle term vanishes because of property 2, and the last term uses property 1. Finally, since δk(k − 1)/k = xk − Mk, the correction can be written as a product of the "old" and the "new" deviation, which is the form usually called Welford's formula:

Sk = Sk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk)

with M0 = 0, S0 = 0. For k = 1 it gives M1 = x1 and S1 = 0, which is correct. Note that the correction is always ≥ 0 (it is δ²(k−1)/k), so Sk can never decrease and can never become negative, not even with rounding errors.

Check with the other route (Σx² form)

Using Sk = Σxi² − k·Mk², we get Sk − Sk−1 = xk² − k Mk² + (k − 1)Mk−1². Substituting xk = M + δ and Mk = M + δ/k (with M = Mk−1):

M² + 2Mδ + δ² − kM² − 2Mδ − δ²/k + (k − 1)M² = δ² − δ²/k = δ²(k − 1)/k

Same result. (This route is fine on paper, but using Σx² in a program is exactly what goes wrong numerically, see 1.3.)

Step 2: recurrence for the variance

Dividing the SS recurrence by k − 1 (for k ≥ 2) and using Sk−1 = (k − 2)·s²k−1:

s²k = ((k − 2)/(k − 1))·s²k−1 + (xk − Mk−1)² / k

and for the "population" version with denominator k, σ̂²k = Sk/k = ((k − 1)/k)·σ̂²k−1 + (k − 1)(xk − Mk−1)²/k². In practice it's simpler to update Sk and divide only when the variance is needed, so that's what the code does.

Step through it

1.2The code

The core logic is three lines.

Python
mean, ss = 0.0, 0.0
for k, x in enumerate(data, start=1):
    d = x - mean          # x_k - M_(k-1)
    mean += d / k         # Knuth
    ss += d * (x - mean)  # Welford

var = ss / (k - 1)        # sample variance
JavaScript (used on this page)
function welfordUpdate(st, x) {
  st.k += 1;
  const d = x - st.mean;
  st.mean += d / st.k;
  st.ss += d * (x - st.mean);
}
// variance = st.ss / (st.k - 1)

Note that in the third line mean has already been updated, so x - mean is xk − Mk while d is still xk − Mk−1. The order of the lines matters. I checked the function against numpy.var(ddof=1) on 1,000 random numbers: the difference was 7·10⁻¹⁵, i.e. just rounding.

1.3Why not just use Σx²?

Numerical stability.

The one-pass formula from the textbook, s² = (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n − 1), is correct mathematically but subtracts two huge numbers that are almost equal. Doubles have about 16 significant digits, so if the data are large compared to their spread, the difference is lost in rounding. Welford never builds these large sums: it only works with deviations from the current mean. The data below always have variance exactly 30 (it's 4, 7, 13, 16 plus a constant):

Try it

With the shift at 10⁸ the naive formula already gives 29.33, at 10⁹ in my browser it gives a negative variance (−170.7), which is impossible, and at 10¹⁰ it gives 21,845. Welford gives 30 every time. (In Python, at 10⁹ I got 0 instead of −170.7: recent versions of Python's sum() use compensated summation, so the rounding is different, but it's still wrong.) The two-pass method (first the mean, then Σ(x − x̄)²) is also accurate, but it needs to go through the data twice. Welford needs one pass and O(1) memory, which fits Ex. 2 well: each hacker's scores arrive one system at a time, and we can update its mean and variance immediately.

2Ex. 2 - Sampling mean and variance

m hackers, n systems each, a score between 0 and 10 for every attempt.

The setup

Extending the previous homework, every penetration attempt now gets a score X ∈ [0, 10] instead of just 0/1. Each of the m hackers attacks n systems and gets a trajectory of n i.i.d. scores X1, …, Xn. For every hacker I run Welford's recurrences along its trajectory and at the end I have its sampling mean and sampling variance

X̄n = Mn,    s²n = Sn/(n − 1).

Then across the m hackers I take the average of the m sampling means and the average of the m sampling variances, and compare them with μ and σ².

The distribution I chose: X = 10·Beta(2, 5)

I wanted something continuous, bounded in [0, 10] and not symmetric: most attempts get a low score (the system mostly holds), and a few go very well. The Beta(2, 5) distribution has exactly this shape, and multiplying by 10 puts it on [0, 10]. Its density is

f(x) = 30·(x/10)(1 − x/10)⁴ / 10,   0 ≤ x ≤ 10

From the Beta formulas (mean a/(a+b), variance ab/((a+b)²(a+b+1)), then ×10 and ×100):

μ = 20/7 ≈ 2.857,   σ² = 125/49 ≈ 2.551

To draw from it I used the fact that Beta(2, 5) is the distribution of the 2nd smallest of 6 independent uniform numbers, so no special library is needed. In the simulator there are also three other distributions to compare with: Uniform(0, 10), Binomial(10, 0.3) (the score is the number of successful steps out of 10, like 10 Bernoulli trials from the last homework) and 10·Beta(½, ½), which is U-shaped ("all or nothing").

Here μ₄ = E[(X − μ)⁴] is the fourth central moment and κ = μ₄/σ⁴ the kurtosis. For 10·Beta(2, 5), μ₄ ≈ 18.74 and κ ≈ 2.88. The reason E[s²] = σ² is the usual one: E[Sn] = Σ E[(Xi − μ)²] − n·E[(X̄ − μ)²] = nσ² − σ² = (n − 1)σ², so we lose one "degree of freedom" because the deviations are measured from X̄ and not from μ.

2.3Average of the sampling means and variances vs μ and σ²

m = 10,000 hackers for each n, X = 10·Beta(2, 5), μ = 2.8571, σ² = 2.5510 (Python).

"z" is the difference from the theoretical value divided by the standard error of the average (sd across hackers / √m). If the estimator is unbiased, z should behave like a standard normal, so values between about −2 and 2 are what we expect.

average of s² (÷ n−1) ± 2 SEaverage of S/n (÷ n)σ² and σ²(n−1)/n

What I conclude

  • The average of the sampling means matches μ for every n, even n = 2. That is because E[X̄n] = μ exactly, for any n: X̄ is an unbiased estimator of μ. The small differences (third decimal) are Monte Carlo noise.
  • The average of the sampling variances matches σ² for every n too, also n = 2, as long as we divide by n − 1. If we divide by n, the average is clearly too small, and exactly by the factor (n − 1)/n: at n = 2 it was 1.229, about half of σ² = 2.551, at n = 10 it was 2.296 ≈ 0.9·σ². This is the reason for Bessel's correction.
  • Almost all the z values are within ±2. The exception is n = 2 where both the mean (z ≈ −2.8) and the variance (z ≈ −2.7) came out a bit low in the same run. I reran n = 2 with 8 other seeds and the z values were all between −2.3 and +1.6, so it's just an unlucky run. It also makes sense that the two came out low together: for a skewed distribution X̄ and s² are not independent, Cov(X̄, s²) = μ₃/n, and with this right-skewed score the correlation across hackers was about +0.29 at n = 2 and 0.43 for large n (theory for large n: 0.435). A hacker with a high average usually also has a high variance, because the high average comes from a few big scores.
  • Unbiased doesn't mean "usually right". At n = 2, 69% of the hackers had s² below σ² and at n = 10 still 56%: the distribution of s² is skewed to the right, so the typical (median) hacker underestimates the variance, and a few hackers overestimate it a lot, balancing the average. The mean of s² is right, the typical value is not.

Why this is useful in inferential statistics

In real life we don't have 10,000 hackers, we have one sample of n observations and we don't know μ or σ². The simulation shows that X̄ and s² computed from that single sample are "right on average", so they are reasonable estimates of the unknown parameters, and it shows how much they can be off (their standard deviation). This is the basis of:

  • Point estimation: X̄ for μ and s² for σ² are unbiased, and both are consistent (their spread goes to 0 as n grows, see 2.4).
  • Standard errors: the precision of X̄ is σ/√n, and since σ is unknown we estimate it with s/√n. This only works because s² estimates σ² correctly.
  • Confidence intervals and tests: the t-interval X̄ ± t0.975, n−1·s/√n combines both estimators. I checked it in the simulation: the fraction of hackers whose interval contained the true μ was 94.2% at n = 2, 93.3% at n = 5, 94.3% at n = 10 and 94.4-95.2% from n = 20 on. So it's close to 95% even though the scores are not normal, and the small undercoverage at small n comes from the skewness.
  • Comparing hackers (or systems): to decide if one hacker is really better than another we need to know how much X̄ varies just by chance. With n = 10 the sampling means of identical hackers already range from about 1.9 to 3.9 (±2 sd), so a "ranking" of hackers based on 10 attempts would be mostly noise.

Showing the Python results (10·Beta(2, 5), m = 10,000).
  • Both spreads go down like 1/√n. On the log-log plot both are straight lines with slope −1/2: multiplying the number of systems by 100 divides the variability by 10. For the means: sd 0.504 at n = 10, 0.051 at n = 1,000. For the variances: 1.17 at n = 10, 0.112 at n = 1,000. The simulation is on top of the theoretical curves.
  • The sampling variance is much noisier than the sampling mean, relatively. At n = 10 the sd of X̄ is 18% of μ, while the sd of s² is 46% of σ². To estimate σ² within ±10% (one sd) we need about n = 200 systems, while for μ the same relative precision is reached with about n = 30. In general sd(s²)/σ² ≈ √((κ − 1)/n), so it depends on the kurtosis of the score distribution: in the simulator the U-shaped 10·Beta(½, ½) has κ = 1.5 and gives much more stable variances, while for a normal distribution (κ = 3) it would be √(2/(n − 1)).
  • The averages over the m hackers are always accurate, even for small n, because averaging over m hackers divides the spread again by √m. So increasing n makes each hacker's estimates more reliable, and increasing m makes the averages across hackers more reliable. In practice, n is what matters if we want to say something about a single hacker.
  • The shape changes too. For small n the distribution of s² is very skewed (see the histogram in 2.2 with n = 2 or 5), and for large n both X̄ and s² become approximately normal (CLT, as in the last homework), centred on μ and σ².

2.5Conclusions

  • Welford's recurrence Sk = Sk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk) follows from Knuth's mean recurrence with a few lines of algebra, needs three lines of code, one pass and constant memory, and doesn't suffer from the cancellation problem of the Σx² formula.
  • Across m = 10,000 simulated hackers, the average of the sampling means agreed with μ = 2.857 and the average of the sampling variances (÷ n − 1) agreed with σ² = 2.551 for every n from 2 to 1,000, within Monte Carlo error. Dividing by n instead gives a bias of exactly −σ²/n.
  • This is what makes X̄ and s² usable as estimates of the unknown μ and σ² from a single sample, and it's the base of standard errors, t-intervals and tests.
  • More systems per hacker make each hacker's mean and variance less variable, both like 1/√n, but the variance estimate stays relatively noisier and more skewed, especially for small n and heavy-tailed scores.
